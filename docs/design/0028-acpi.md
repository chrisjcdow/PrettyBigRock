# 0028: ACPI: the AML interpreter and the ACPI service

Status: Accepted · 25 November 2026, accepted 25 November 2026 · Related: DESIGN.md sections 7.1, 11 and 14; notes 0010,
0011, 0018, 0019, 0024, 0027; `docs/m7-plan.md` (Q2, Q13); milestone M7

## The problem

A PC tells the operating system about itself through **ACPI**, a set of tables the firmware
leaves in memory. The kernel already reads the simple ones, which are fixed-format records: the
MADT (the cores and interrupt controllers), the FADT (power registers), the HPET, the MCFG
(where PCI configuration space is), the SPCR (the serial console) (note 0005).

The rest of what the firmware knows is in the **DSDT** and the **SSDTs**, and those aren't
records: they are programs, in a bytecode called **AML** (ACPI Machine Language), describing a
tree of named objects (the **namespace**: `\_SB.PCI0.LPCB.UAR2` is a serial port under the
chipset's LPC bridge under the first PCI host bridge). Asking a question means running a small
method and reading what it returns. M7 needs three answers that only AML gives:

1. **Which legacy devices exist, and where.** A serial port, the PS/2 keyboard controller and
   other devices not on PCI announce themselves nowhere else. Their `_CRS` ("current resource
   settings") method returns their ports and interrupts. On Roxy, `UAR2`'s `_CRS` reads the
   board's I/O chip at run time to find out, so no table could replace running it. Today the
   device manager has a built-in list instead (note 0011, section 4), which can't know, and on
   Roxy is wrong (the FADT doesn't set its "8042 present" flag, so PS/2 isn't started; note
   0027, section 5).
2. **How PCI interrupt lines are wired.** A device without MSI (Roxy's two USB controllers)
   raises one of four pins, and the board routes each to an interrupt controller line. Only the
   `_PRT` ("PCI routing table") method says where, and only after the operating system has
   called `\_PIC(1)` to say it uses I/O APICs rather than the 1980s PIC.
3. **How to turn the machine off.** Writing two values from the `\_S5` object to the power
   registers. The power service finds them today by searching the table's bytes for `_S5_`, a
   guess labelled as a stopgap until M7 (note 0024, section 7).

Chris chose (Q2, 23 November 2026) to write our own interpreter rather than borrow ACPICA (C,
used by Linux, FreeBSD and Windows-alike systems) or the Rust `aml` crate, as DESIGN.md section
14's rule says, built only as far as M7 needs. This note designs it, the service that runs it,
and what changes around it. It is the largest single piece of M7.

AML is the firmware's code, written by Foxconn and AMI for Roxy, and it runs with the power to
touch hardware. It must be treated two ways at once: as **trusted** in what it does to the
machine (it is the firmware; it can already do anything through the processor's system
management mode), and as **untrusted input** to our interpreter, which must never crash, hang
or be steered outside the machine's devices by malformed or malicious bytecode, as `lib/devices`
treats PCI configuration space (note 0011, section 3).

## The design

### 1. Two parts: `lib/aml` and `services/acpi`

- **`lib/aml`** (`pbr-aml`, `no_std` with `alloc`): the interpreter. It knows nothing about
  PrettyBigRock: everything it needs from the machine (reading a register, sleeping, the time)
  comes through a `Host` trait the caller provides. So it is built and tested on the host as
  well as in the system, like `lib/rockfs` and `lib/devices`.
- **`services/acpi`**: a service that runs it. It holds the device access AML needs (section
  4), loads the tables once at start, and answers questions over a new interface, `pbr.acpi`
  (section 6). The kernel never interprets AML (note 0019 already ruled it out).

Putting the interpreter in a service, not in the device manager, keeps its bugs away from the
program that holds every device and DMA capability (it gets no `DMA` right, and no interrupts
but its own), lets it be restarted on its own, and gives the power service something to ask
without depending on the device manager.

### 2. The interpreter (`lib/aml`)

**What it reads.** The DSDT and every SSDT the root table lists, in order, into one namespace,
as the ACPI specification (6.5, which this note follows) says. Each table's checksum and length
are checked; a table that fails is skipped and reported, not fatal. The integer width is 64
bits, or 32 if the DSDT's revision is below 2.

**What it understands.** Everything in the specification's AML grammar except what section 9
leaves out. In groups:
- **the namespace**: `Scope`, `Name`, `Alias`, `Device`, `Method`, `Processor` (the old form),
  `PowerResource`, `ThermalZone`, `Event`, `Mutex`, `External`;
- **data**: integers, strings, buffers, packages (including variable-length), references, and
  the constants (`Zero`, `One`, `Ones`, `Revision`);
- **hardware access**: `OperationRegion`, `Field`, `IndexField`, `BankField` with every access
  type, lock rule and update rule; `CreateBitField` to `CreateQWordField` and `CreateField` on
  buffers;
- **methods**: arguments, locals, return values, `Serialized` methods and their sync levels;
- **control**: `If`/`Else`, `While`, `Break`, `Continue`, `Return`, `Noop`, `BreakPoint` (a
  no-op), `Fatal` (logged, then the method fails);
- **expressions**: arithmetic and logic, `Store`, `CopyObject`, `RefOf`, `CondRefOf`,
  `DerefOf`, `Index`, `SizeOf`, `ObjectType`, `Match`, `Concatenate`, `ConcatenateResTemplate`,
  `Mid`, the `To…` conversions, `FindSetLeftBit` and the like, and the implicit conversions
  the specification lists;
- **synchronisation and time**: `Acquire`/`Release` (including the global lock, section 4),
  `Sleep`, `Stall`, `Timer`, `Wait`/`Signal`/`Reset` on events;
- **`Notify`**: recorded and passed to the host (section 5 says what M7 does with it);
- **`_OSI`**, **`_OS`** and **`_REV`**, built in. Firmware is written and tested against
  Windows, and takes its most-tested path when told it is running on Windows, so `_OSI` answers
  true for every Windows version string ACPICA knows (from `"Windows 2000"` to
  `"Windows 2022"`) and false for anything else, `"Linux"` included, as Linux does. `_OS` is
  `"Microsoft Windows NT"`, and `_REV` is 2.

  Roxy shows why. Its `\_SB.PCI0._INI` sets a variable `OSYS` to 2000, then raises it for
  each of seven Windows names that `_OSI` says yes to (2001 to 2012); it never asks about any
  other name. With `OSYS` at 2000 the HPET device's `_STA` says "not present", the 64-bit PCI
  memory window is left out of the root bridge's resources, and the Vista-and-later paths are
  skipped. Firmware can only ask about names its makers knew, so a name of our own would never
  be asked about.

**How it runs.** Tables are parsed into the namespace once. Method bodies are kept as bytes and
parsed as they run, because AML's grammar depends on the namespace at that moment (how many
arguments a name takes is only known by looking it up). Everything is bounds-checked; nothing in
`lib/aml` uses `unsafe`, indexes without checking, or recurses without a depth limit.

**Limits, so bad bytecode can't hang or exhaust the service:**
- a method call nests at most 64 deep;
- one evaluation (a question asked of the namespace, and every method it calls) runs at most
  10 million operations and at most 30 seconds by the host's clock (as long as the
  specification lets `Sleep` and `Stall` take; ACPICA's limits are of the same size). Past
  either, it is abandoned with an error, and the namespace changes it made stay, as they would
  after a firmware bug on any system;
- objects the namespace creates are counted against a limit (16 MiB of the service's budget);
- a string, buffer or package can't be made longer than 1 MiB.

**Results it returns** are plain data (integers, strings, buffers, packages of them), and the
library decodes the two kinds of result M7 asks for:
- **resource templates** (`_CRS`): I/O ports (`IO`, `FixedIO`), interrupts (`IRQ`,
  `IRQNoFlags`, `Interrupt`), memory (`Memory24`, `Memory32`, `Memory32Fixed`) and the
  address-space descriptors (`WordIO`, `DWordMemory`, `QWordMemory` and the like, which PCI
  host bridges use). Each is length-checked; an unknown descriptor is skipped by its length;
  a template that doesn't end properly is an error;
- **routing tables** (`_PRT`): packages of (device address, pin, source, source index), where
  the source is either 0 (the index is the line itself) or a reference to an interrupt link
  device, whose own `_CRS` gives the line.

### 3. Starting up

The ACPI service, in order:
1. **Reads the tables.** The machine description (note 0011, section 2) gives the root pointer;
   the service maps the tables through its device memory capability, as the device manager
   reads the MCFG today.
2. **Puts the machine in ACPI mode** (x86-64). Old firmware starts in "legacy mode", where its
   own hidden handler (SMM) deals with power events and the ACPI registers are switched off. If
   the FADT names an SMI command port and `SCI_EN` (bit 0 of PM1 control) is clear, the service
   writes the FADT's `ACPI_ENABLE` value to that port and waits up to 3 seconds for `SCI_EN` to
   come on. Every ACPI operating system does this; it's also what makes the power button an
   event we can see (section 5).
3. **Loads the namespace** (section 2), then evaluates `\_PIC(1)` if it exists (1 meaning I/O
   APICs; on ARM64 there is no `\_PIC`).
4. **Initialises devices**, as the specification says: `\_SB._INI` if it exists, then for every
   device, depth first, its `_STA` (present, and working), and its `_INI` if it is present.
   A device that isn't present is skipped with everything under it, unless `_STA` says it is
   "functioning" (the specification's case for a device whose children may still be there).
5. **Turns the events off** that M7 doesn't handle: every general-purpose event (the GPE
   blocks' enable registers written to zero, their status cleared), and every fixed event but
   the power button (section 5).
6. **Serves** `pbr.acpi`, and logs one line: how many tables, objects and devices, and
   anything skipped.

`_OSC` (asking the firmware to hand over PCI Express features) and `_PDC` (processor power
states, which load Roxy's six dynamic SSDTs) aren't called in M7: without them, the firmware
keeps those features and the processor stays in its simple power states, which is safe.

### 4. Access: what the service holds, and what AML may touch

**What it is started with** (by the root program, which holds all device access, note 0010,
section 8):
- the **machine description**;
- a **device memory** capability for the whole address space, **without `DMA`**;
- on x86-64, the **I/O ports** capability, 0 to 65535;
- the **SCI**: one interrupt line, the FADT's `SCI_INT`, with `BIND` and `ACKNOWLEDGE`;
- PCI configuration space it reaches through device memory, at the MCFG's regions (on x86-64
  without an MCFG, through ports `0xCF8` and `0xCFC`).

**Why so broad.** An `OperationRegion` can be declared anywhere (Roxy's DSDT has 46, in system
memory, I/O ports and PCI configuration space: the chipset's power registers, the I/O chip's
configuration ports, the firmware's own memory (`GNVS`), the port that calls the firmware's
system management mode), and a method can create one at an address it computes. Nothing short
of running the AML says what it will need. The kernel still refuses the two things that
matter most (note 0010): **ordinary memory** (no device memory capability reaches a page the
kernel manages) and **the kernel's own devices** (interrupt controllers, HPET, the console's
port). The service's own rules add:
- **Ports Windows protects from AML are refused**: the old DMA controllers, the PIC and its
  trigger registers (`0x4D0`, `0x4D1`), the timer, the clock and CMOS (`0x70` to `0x76`), port
  `0x92`, and the PCI configuration ports. ACPICA keeps the same list, since firmware is tested
  against Windows and never relies on them.
- Any refused access, by the kernel or by this list, is **ignored**: a read gives 0, a write
  does nothing, and it is logged once per region. The method carries on, as it would on
  Windows.
- Every region's first access is logged in verbose mode (`acpi=verbose` on the command line):
  its name, kind and range, so on Roxy we can see exactly what the firmware touches.

**What this gives.** A bug in our interpreter, or in the firmware, can disturb devices, but
not the kernel or any program's memory; and the service holds nothing else a broken interpreter
could leak or misuse (no files, no DMA). That is the same line DESIGN.md section 7.1 draws for
drivers. AML is the firmware's own code and could already do anything through system
management mode, so restricting it further would protect nothing and break firmware.

**The global lock.** Some firmware shares data with its hidden handler and guards it with a
lock in the FACS table (`\_GL`). `Acquire(\_GL)` takes that lock by the specification's
protocol (compare-and-swap on the FACS's lock word); if the firmware holds it, the service
waits, polling every millisecond, within the method's time limit, and on release sets
`GBL_RLS` in PM1 control if the firmware asked to be told.

### 5. Events: the power button

With ACPI mode on, pressing the power button sets `PWRBTN_STS` in the PM1 status register and
raises the **SCI** (the ACPI interrupt; line 9 on Roxy, whose SMI command port is `0xB2`). The service binds the SCI, enables only
the power button's fixed event (`PWRBTN_EN`), and when it fires, clears the status (by writing
1 to it) and signals whoever asked through `watch_button` (section 6). The power service passes
it on to the users service, which shuts down exactly as when the person chooses Turn off from
the menu (note 0024, section 7). Holding the button down for four seconds still cuts the power,
which the board does by itself.

That needs the FADT to say the power button is a **fixed** one (its flag `PWR_BUTTON` clear),
as Roxy's and QEMU's q35's are. A "control-method" button, and every other event (lids,
batteries, docking, hot-plug `Notify`), needs the general-purpose events and their `_Lxx` and
`_Exx` methods: M11. `Notify` calls made by methods M7 does run are logged and dropped. On
ARM64, QEMU's `virt` machine reports its power button through a different device (the "generic
event device"); that waits too, and ARM64 machines are turned off from the menu.

### 6. The interface: `pbr.acpi`

```
interface pbr.acpi version 1;

/// A range of I/O ports.
struct Ports { first: u16, count: u16 }
/// A range of physical memory.
struct Range { base: u64, length: u64 }
/// An interrupt line, as the firmware describes it.
struct Line {
    /// x86-64: the global system interrupt; ARM64: the GIC's interrupt ID.
    number: u32,
    /// Level-triggered (otherwise edge).
    level: bool,
    /// Active low (otherwise high).
    active_low: bool,
}

/// A device the namespace describes that is present, has a hardware ID, and isn't a PCI
/// function (those are found on the bus, note 0011).
struct Device {
    /// Its full name, such as `\_SB.PCI0.LPCB.UAR2`.
    path: string<128>,
    /// `_HID`, as text (`PNP0501`; an EISA number is converted, as the specification says).
    hid: string<16>,
    /// `_CID`, its compatible IDs.
    compatible: list<string<16>, 4>,
    /// `_UID`, if it has one (as text: some firmware gives a string).
    uid: string<16>,
    /// What `_CRS` returned.
    ports: list<Ports, 8>,
    memory: list<Range, 8>,
    lines: list<Line, 4>,
}

/// The devices, from `first` (0 to start); `next` is where to continue, if there are more.
@1 fn devices(first: u32) -> (devices: list<Device, 16>, next: option<u32>) error AcpiError;

/// Where a PCI function's interrupt pin is wired. `pin` is 1 to 4 (INTA to INTD), as the
/// function's configuration space says.
@2 fn route(segment: u16, bus: u8, device: u8, pin: u8) -> (line: Line) error AcpiError;

/// Turns the machine off (x86-64). Answers only if it can't.
@3 fn off() -> () error AcpiError;

/// Signals `bits` on `note` each time the power button is pressed. Replaces any earlier watch.
@4 fn watch_button(note: handle<notification>, bits: u64) -> () error AcpiError;

flexible enum AcpiError {
    /// No such device, no route for that pin, or no `\_S5`.
    NotFound @1,
    /// Not allowed through this capability.
    NotAllowed @2,
    /// The firmware's method failed or ran too long (the log says which).
    Failed @3,
    /// This machine can't do it this way (ARM64's `off`; no fixed power button).
    NotSupported @4,
}
```

**Who may call what**, by badge, as the root program's lookup table decides (note 0018): the
device manager (`acpi`, badge `DEVICES`) may call `devices` and `route`; the power service
(`acpi`, badge `POWER`) may call `off` and `watch_button`. Anything else is `NotAllowed`.

**`route`** finds the namespace device for the function's bus: the host bridge (`_HID` or
`_CID` `PNP0A03` or `PNP0A08`) whose `_BBN` (base bus number, 0 if absent) is that bus, or a
PCI-to-PCI bridge (a device whose `_ADR` names a bridge function) whose secondary bus number,
read from its configuration space, is that bus. If that device has a `_PRT`, the entry for
(device, pin − 1) is the answer: the line itself when the source is 0, which ACPI defines as
level-triggered and active low; otherwise the link device's `_CRS`, with the trigger and
polarity it states. If the bridge has no `_PRT`, the pin is "swizzled" to the bridge's own pin,
as the PCI-to-PCI bridge specification wires it (`(pin − 1 + device) mod 4`), and the search
goes up a bus. A link with no line set (its `_CRS` empty) is `NotFound`: setting one with
`_SRS` isn't done in M7, since QEMU's and Roxy's links in APIC mode are fixed.

**`off`** evaluates `\_PTS(5)` if it exists (the firmware's "prepare to sleep"), reads `\_S5`'s
two sleep-type values, and writes `SLP_TYPa | SLP_EN` to PM1a control and `SLP_TYPb | SLP_EN`
to PM1b control if there is one (bits 10 to 12 and bit 13), as 1.3 and today's stopgap do. If
the machine is still running 3 seconds later, it answers `Failed`.

### 7. What changes around it

- **The root program** starts the ACPI service before the device manager, supervised like the
  others (note 0018): a new service kind, `acpi`, with a 24 MiB budget. Its lookup table gains
  `(devmgr, "acpi") → DEVICES` and `(power, "acpi") → POWER`. A machine without ACPI (none yet;
  the Raspberry Pi in M10 uses a device tree) doesn't start it.
- **The device manager** (note 0011 changes):
  - the **built-in list goes**: COM2 and PS/2 are found by asking `devices`. Driver manifests
    gain **`match acpi <ID>`**, matched against a device's `_HID` and then its `_CID`s:
    `drivers/serial16550` matches `PNP0501`; `drivers/ps2` matches `PNP0303` (the keyboard
    controller), and is also given the line of the `PNP0F13` device (the mouse port) if there
    is one, since one controller serves both. A device whose ports the kernel owns (the
    kernel's console, note 0027, section 3) is skipped and logged, since slicing them is
    refused;
  - for each PCI function that **uses a line** (it has no MSI, or its manifest asks for a line),
    it reads the function's interrupt pin, asks `route`, sets the line's trigger and polarity
    with `interrupt_configure` (section 8), and gives the driver a one-line slice. Several
    functions routed to one line share it (note 0027, section 2);
  - if the ACPI service isn't there or has been given up on (note 0018's restart policy), the
    device manager carries on with what needs no AML: PCI devices with MSI. It logs what it
    couldn't do. There is no fallback list: a second way of finding devices would be a second
    thing to keep right.
  - the machine description's PS/2 flag is no longer used (it stays in the format, ignored).
- **The power service** (note 0024, section 7, changes): on x86-64, `off` asks the ACPI service
  instead of searching the tables; its stopgap file is removed, and the root program no longer
  gives it the PM1 ports and table slices. It calls `watch_button` at start and, when the button
  is pressed, signals the users service. `pbr.power` gains, for the users service alone:
  ```
  /// Signals `bits` on `note` each time the power button is pressed.
  @3 fn watch_button(note: handle<notification>, bits: u64) -> () error PowerError;
  ```
  and the users service, told, does what `power_off` does. Restarting is unchanged (the kernel,
  note 0019).
- **The kernel** gains one call (section 8).

### 8. A kernel addition: setting a line's trigger and polarity

Note 0027 (section 2) left the kernel choosing every line's trigger and polarity itself
(active low and level for PCI's lines), and put off a call to set them until a machine needed
it. QEMU's q35 does: its `_PRT` routes through link devices (`GSIA` to `GSIH`) whose `_CRS`
says **active high**, and a line programmed the wrong way round looks permanently raised, so it
fires for ever (KVM's model of the I/O APIC, which Impala's boot tests use, takes the
polarity into account). Roxy's names its
lines directly, which means active low. So the right setting comes from AML, line by line, and
the device manager must be able to tell the kernel. This joins interface version 1.4, which
isn't built yet:

- **New call 89, `interrupt_configure(h, index, mode)`**: needs the new interrupt right
  **`CONFIGURE`** (type bit 10). `mode` bit 0 is **level-triggered** (otherwise edge), bit 1 is
  **active low** (otherwise high); other bits must be 0 (`INVALID_ARGUMENT`).
- It applies to **line** interrupts only (message-signalled ones are `INVALID_ARGUMENT`), and
  only while **nobody has bound the line** (`BAD_STATE`), so sharers always agree. The setting
  stays until changed, through unbinding and binding again.
- On ARM64 the GIC can't take active-low lines: `mode` with bit 1 set is `NOT_SUPPORTED`.
- Until it is called, a line keeps the kernel's default (note 0027, section 2).
- The root program's interrupt capability has `CONFIGURE` (it has every right). The device
  manager keeps it, and slices its drivers get never have it.

### 9. Not in M7

- **General-purpose events** and their methods (`_Lxx`, `_Exx`, `_Qxx`), and with them
  `Notify` handling, the **embedded controller** (laptops' keyboard, battery and lid chip), the
  control-method power button, lids and batteries: M11.
- **Sleep** (`\_S3`, waking): M11.
- **`Load` and `LoadTable`**, which add tables while running: they answer failure. Roxy's
  three `Load`s are in SSDT3, its processor power-state table, which loads the dynamic SSDTs
  from methods M7 doesn't call.
- **Setting resources** (`_SRS`, `_PRS`), `_OSC`, `_PDC`, `_DSM`, thermal zones, processor
  power and performance states.
- **The SMBus, embedded controller, CMOS, PCI BAR, IPMI, GPIO and serial-bus regions.** A
  region of another kind reads as 0 and ignores writes, logged once. None appears in Roxy's
  tables.

### 10. Testing

**On the host** (`cargo xtask test`), in `lib/aml`:
- **Small programs**: ASL sources in `lib/aml/tests/asl/`, each checking one part of the
  language (fields of every access type, buffer fields, package references, implicit
  conversions, `Serialized`, `While` with `Break`, the limits), compiled with ACPICA's `iasl`
  and committed as AML beside their source, so the tests run without `iasl`. `iasl` is only
  needed to change one.
- **Real tables**: QEMU's q35 and `virt` (written by a new `cargo xtask acpidump`, which boots
  the kernel with `acpidump` on its command line; the kernel writes every table to the serial
  log in hexadecimal, and the tool saves them under `tests/data/acpi/qemu-q35/` and
  `qemu-virt/`), and Roxy's (`tests/data/acpi/roxy-h61mx/`). With a fake `Host` that remembers
  register values, each is loaded, initialised as in section 3, and every device's `_STA`,
  `_HID`, `_UID` and `_CRS`, every `_PRT`, and `\_S5` evaluated. The results are checked
  against expected answers kept beside the tables (for Roxy: the serial port at `0x2F8`, line 3,
  as Linux says; the USB controllers on lines 16 and 23).
- **Against ACPICA**: when `acpiexec` (ACPICA's tool for running tables outside a machine, in
  Ubuntu's `acpica-tools`) is installed, the same evaluations are run through it and the two
  must agree, as RockFS is checked against `tools/rockfs.py`. Skipped without it, as the FAT32
  tests are without `mtools`; a stage isn't finished until they've run, and CI installs it.
- **Bad input**: every real table and test program, randomly corrupted (bytes changed,
  lengths cut, packages nested deeply, loops made endless), must load or fail cleanly and every
  evaluation must end within the limits, with no panic.

**In QEMU** (all four configurations): the ACPI service starts and logs its summary; the
program self-test checks `pbr.acpi` (each badge allowed only its calls, `devices` paging,
`route` for a function, `interrupt_configure`'s rules from section 8); the device manager finds
PS/2 and COM2 through AML on x86-64; and the desktop boot's last step turns the machine off
through `off` (it already checks QEMU's `guest-shutdown`). Pressing the power button is QEMU's
`system_powerdown` command, which the desktop boot sends in place of the menu's Turn off on one
x86-64 configuration (note 0031 chooses), so both ways are covered. The INTx test configuration (note
0031) uses `route` for real.

**On Roxy** (stages 1 to 3): the service's verbose log on COM2, compared with what Linux says
(`/proc/interrupts`, `lspci -vv`); PS/2 found; the power button turning Roxy off cleanly.

## Alternatives considered

- **Borrowing ACPICA or the `aml` crate**: settled by Q2 (DESIGN.md section 14's rule).
  ACPICA is still used, outside the system, to check ours.
- **The interpreter in the device manager.** One fewer service, but the program holding every
  DMA capability would also parse firmware bytecode, and the power service would depend on the
  device manager.
- **Granting the service only the regions the tables declare**, sliced one by one by the
  device manager as AML asks. Regions are created at run time at computed addresses, the
  firmware could already reach any of them through system management mode, and the kernel
  already keeps it out of memory and the kernel's devices; the extra machinery would protect
  nothing.
- **Answering `_OSI("Linux")` true**, or nothing at all. Firmware written for Windows takes its
  least-tested paths for anything else; Linux learnt this and says no to "Linux".
- **Answering as `"PrettyBigRock"`.** No firmware asks about it, so it would behave as for an
  unknown system (Roxy: as for Windows 2000, see the interpreter section above). Answering like Windows copies no
  one's code; it only answers the firmware's question the way it was tested.
- **Keeping the built-in device list as a fallback.** Two ways to find the same devices, one
  of them guessing; and on Roxy the guess is wrong anyway.
- **Polling the power button**, or ignoring it. Without ACPI mode, a press makes the firmware
  cut the power at once, losing whatever wasn't saved.
- **Leaving the polarity to the kernel's default** (note 0027's choice). Right on Roxy, wrong
  on QEMU, so wrong somewhere for certain.

## Open questions

- **Harriet's tables**, as a third real machine for the host tests: on Windows they can be
  copied with ACPICA's `acpidump.exe`. Not needed for M7.
- `_OSC`, so PCI Express hot-plug and error reporting can be ours rather than the firmware's
  (M11).
- The ARM64 power button (the generic event device), with the general-purpose events (M11).
- Restarting the service runs `_INI` methods again, which firmware doesn't expect. Nothing has
  shown this to be harmful; if it is, the service could remember across restarts (the root
  program keeps a note) that they ran.
- Reassigning PCI resources the firmware didn't assign (note 0011): only if Roxy needs it.

- 28 November 2026, stage 2 (`lib/aml` built):
  - **The stack.** The interpreter recurses as it reads; unoptimised, one level of an
    expression took over 20 KiB of stack (every local of a large `match` gets its own slot), so
    `lib/aml` is built optimised even in debug builds, where a level takes about 1.1 KiB and the
    1024 levels the nesting limit allows need about 1.2 MiB. The ACPI service has a 4 MiB stack;
    the tests run the deep cases on 2 MiB.
  - **Memory** is counted per evaluation (16 MiB of new strings, buffers and packages), and
    for loading the tables, rather than as a running total of the namespace: the names a method
    creates are removed when it returns, so after loading the namespace holds only what the
    tables declared, and each value stored in it is at most 1 MiB.
  - **`_REG`**, missing from section 3: after `\_SB._INI` and before the devices' `_STA` and
    `_INI`, each device with regions of system memory, I/O or PCI configuration space has its
    `_REG(space, 1)` run, as ACPICA does, since firmware often waits for it before touching a
    region.
  - **A region declared at a table's top level** has its offset and length worked out at its
    first use, as ACPICA does: they may name objects defined further on.
  - **`_OSI`** also says yes to `"Extended Address Space Descriptor"`, as ACPICA always does
    (the library decodes that descriptor).
  - **Mutexes** never wait, since one evaluation runs at a time; only the global lock can be
    held by someone else (the firmware). So `Serialized` changes nothing here.
  - Roxy's tables give Linux's answers: COM2 at `0x2F8` on line 3 (its `_CRS` reads the board's
    ITE I/O chip, which the tests' fake machine imitates), the EHCI controllers on lines 16 and
    23, and `\_S5` 7. Its PS/2 devices' `_STA` reads a variable the firmware sets in memory at
    start, so whether PS/2 is there is only known on Roxy.
- 28 November 2026, stage 2 (the service and what changes around it):
  - **QEMU's tables** come from `cargo xtask acpidump`, booted with the boot tests' devices
    (a second serial port included, or q35's DSDT has no COM2).
  - **The device manager's wired lines for PCI functions** (section 7's second point) move to
    stage 4, with Roxy's EHCI controllers, the first devices that need them and the
    configuration that tests them; `route` itself is built and tested here (the program
    self-test asks it for device 1's INTA on both architectures).
  - **The PS/2 driver** runs with the keyboard port's line alone when the firmware lists no

  - **The power button**: the ACPI service logs "the power button was pressed", the power
    service passes it on, and the users service turns the computer off as Turn off does. The
    x86-64 UEFI desktop boot ends with QEMU's `system_powerdown`.
  - **Labels**: a device is logged by its own name in the namespace (`com2`, `kbd`, `uar2`).
- 28 November 2026, stage 2 (the cross-check against ACPICA, version 20251212): every device's
  `_STA`, `_HID`, `_CID`, `_UID`, `_CRS` and `_PRT`, and `\_S5`, on all three machines, agree
  (389 evaluations; ACPICA's debugger can't print two of q35's and virt's `_PRT`s, too big for
  its buffer). The small programs (`lib/aml/tests/asl/`, 23 tests in four files) found three
  places where this interpreter differed, now as ACPICA does them:
  - a field made by **`CreateField`** reads as a buffer whatever its length (`CreateByteField`
    and its kin as integers), as Windows does;
  - **`ToHexString`** of an integer is `0x` and its digits without leading zeros (`"0x1A"`);
    the implicit conversion keeps all 16 digits;
  - **`DerefOf`** of a package element that names an object gives that object's value, and a
    device, having none, is an error; `ObjectType` of such an element is the named object's.
  `acpiexec` is fed its commands on standard input: its `-b` silently ignores a batch longer
  than about 1000 characters. CI installs `acpica-tools`, but Ubuntu 24.04's is version
  20230628, whose `ToHexString` still wrote 16 digits: the small programs are compared only
  with 20251212 or newer (on Impala), the real machines' tables with any version (in CI too).
